# Vocabulary and phrases

Read this file before you change, keep, or flag a particular word or phrase. It holds Section C of `natural_academic_writing_guide.md` in full, the vocabulary evidence from `AI Detection.md` Sections 6.2 and 6.3, every additional evaluative phrase that appears only inside the guide's worked examples, a claim-strength table, and the rules for punctuation and house-style word lists.

**This is not a list of prohibited words.** Every word below has legitimate uses. A word is weak only when, in this sentence, it fails to supply an accurate, supported meaning.

## 1. What vocabulary research supports

| Finding | Level | What it supports | What it does not support |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kobak and colleagues studied more than 15 million biomedical abstracts and found unusual increases in selected stylistic words after widespread LLM adoption, including *delves*, *underscores*, *showcasing*, and already common words such as *potential*, *findings*, and *crucial* [18] | [R] | A population-level lower-bound estimate of LLM-assisted abstracts | The origin of any individual sentence or paper. Grammatical variants need careful counting, so treating each form as an independent clue misrepresents the analysis. |
| The Wikipedia community guide lists further words (Section 3 below) [26] | [C] | Words an editor may want to look at twice | Turnitin's rules or any commercial detector's published behavior |
| No exhaustive trigger-word list appears in the reviewed official sources [4] | [V], [E] | Skepticism toward any "words Turnitin flags" list | A promise that removing words produces any score |

A word can be frequent in one corpus, appropriate in a particular paper, and uninformative in another. Evaluate meaning, context, frequency, purpose, and contribution together.

## 2. The decision sequence

Apply these steps to any word you are considering changing:

1. Identify the intended meaning.
2. Check whether the word supplies that meaning.
3. Locate any evidence the word implies ("robust" implies a stress test, "enduring" implies a duration).
4. Examine nearby repetition.
5. Decide: **keep** (meaning is exact and supported), **specify** (name what the word gestures at, from facts already present), **replace** (a plainer word carries the same meaning), **delete** (the word adds evaluation without information), or **query** (the meaning depends on facts you do not have).

Do not swap a precise word because it appears in this file. Where information is missing, a query is more honest than a synonym.

## 3. Vocabulary inventory

| showcase, showcasing | K, W | Present selected examples or capabilities, often in a demonstration | It turns analysis into promotion. | enduring | W | Continuing over a stated or meaningful period | Follow-up is short or absent. | landscape | W | A physical scene, or a metaphorical overview of a field | It substitutes for the actual institutions, methods, or changes. Which elements are included? | "The landscape map distinguishes forest, farmland, and settlement." | "The changing research landscape demands action." → identify the specific change and decision, or remove the claim. |
| meticulously | W | With great care and attention to detail | It is self-praise for an unspecified procedure. What verifiable checks were performed? | "The diary describes the archivist working meticulously. | pivotal | W | Central to a specified turning point or decision | The claimed influence is undefined. What depends on it? | "The missing date is pivotal to deciding which of the two timelines is possible." | "This pivotal study compares two models." → "This study compares two models," unless wider influence is established. |

The table covers every distinct lexical item in Source A Sections 6.2 and 6.3, with variant forms grouped (24 items: 21 from the Wikipedia list, plus *potential*, *findings*, and *crucial* from the corpus study). Not every use requires replacement. "Potential" in physics and "findings" in a results discussion may be exactly right. Even "valuable" is informative when its use and reason are stated.

## 4. Phrase families

| Phrase family | Legitimate function | Weak use and contribution test | Revision approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| "In today's changing..." | Establish a relevant temporal change | Generic scene-setting: what changed, and why does it matter here? | Replace with the known change. If none is supplied, begin with the research question. |
| "Represents a major advance" | State a contribution relative to prior work | Unsupported scale: an advance on which criterion and comparator? | "The study compares A and B" keeps a documented action without inventing novelty. |
| "By enhancing the overall workflow" | Explain a process improvement | Circular explanation: does the phrase identify an operation? | State the actual operation (for example, copying a supplied value between forms) only when documented. |
| "Not merely X, but Y" | Correct a real category error or distinguish scope | Empty contrast: what changes in interpretation? | Keep the distinction if substantive. Otherwise state the known function directly. |
| "Flexible, efficient, and effective" | Summarize distinct evaluated properties | Rhythmic praise: are the three properties separately defined and supported? | Report the defined capabilities or measured outcomes. Do not substitute new adjectives. |
| "Despite these challenges, further work..." | Connect an actual limitation to an open question | Generic limitation: which challenge constrains which inference? | "The single-site evaluation leaves transferability unresolved" builds on a supplied design limitation. |
| "Researchers agree" | Summarize a documented consensus | Vague attribution: which sources, criteria, and exceptions? | Attribute the finding to verified sources and preserve disagreement where relevant. |
| "Clearly demonstrates the importance" | Explain an implication when the reasoning is supplied | Empty reinforcement: what new proposition does it add? | Delete it, or state the supported implication explicitly. |
| "May perhaps potentially suggest" | Express uncertainty | Redundant qualification: which hedge adds a distinct limit? | "May suggest" is a local repair. Then explain the source of uncertainty. |
| "Ensures reliable performance everywhere" | State a valid formal guarantee within assumptions | Universalization: where is the proof or exhaustive evidence? | Name evaluated conditions and outcome. Never recast a limited test as universal reliability. |
| "Available sources offer insights" | Describe a source's role in an annotated review | Source filler: which proposition is supported? | "The guide defines qualifying text" names a concrete contribution. |
| "These findings are promising" | State a cautious, evidence-based prospect | Repeated conclusion: what distinct next step follows from this finding? | End with the result, its scope, or a justified next question. |

## 6. Claim-strength vocabulary

These words assert something about evidence. Each needs the support named in the third column. When support is narrower, state the observation and its boundary, as the guide's examples do. Do not reach for a fixed weaker synonym, and never weaken a directly established result. [E]

| Word or construction | What it asserts | Evidence required | Guide's repair when evidence is narrower |
|---|---|---|---|
| caused, because, led to, drives | Causation | A design that isolates the effect | "was associated with," plus the design limitation (E5, I6, G2) |
| proves, establishes | Conclusive demonstration | A formal proof or a decisive design | State the observation and what it does not establish (E5, I6) |
| ensures, guarantees | Certainty of an outcome | A proof within stated assumptions, or exhaustive evaluation | Name the evaluated conditions (B14, I7) |
| all, every, any, in every environment | Universality | Exhaustive evidence | State the tested set (B14, D8) |
| in practice, real-world, deployment-ready | Operational validity | A deployment evaluation | "in this benchmark," "deployment remains untested" (B05, G1) |
| significantly | Statistical significance | A named test and result | Report the test, or remove the word (B02) |
| unprecedented, groundbreaking, first, novel | Novelty | A literature search or direct comparison | Describe the documented contribution (B02, I2) |
| robust, reliable, effective, efficient, comprehensive | An evaluated property | A stated criterion and test | Define the criterion and report the evidence, or remove the word (K checklist) |
| superior, outperforms, best | A comparison | A comparison on a named outcome | Name the comparator and outcome, or recast as the open question (B10, I9) |
| "established no effect," "showed no difference" | Evidence of absence | A test powered to detect the effect, with an interval excluding it | "did not establish," "the interval includes no difference" (G1, Pass 8) |

Keep the distinction between "did not establish" and "established no." A nonsignificant result is not proof of no effect.

## 7. Punctuation and house-style word lists

- **Punctuation proves nothing about provenance.** Wikipedia's advice page records heavy em-dash use as a historical observation, and the source treats it as contextual. [26] [C]. Choose punctuation for meaning, readability, and the applicable style requirements.
- **House style governs when supplied.** If the user, venue, or project supplies style rules (for example: no em dashes, no semicolons, American spelling, or a list of words to avoid), apply them to every sentence you write or change. Treat them as style requirements, never as detection tactics.
- **Meaning wins a conflict.** If a house-style rule would change meaning, satisfy the rule by rephrasing so the meaning survives. Examples: a banned hedge such as "could" that carries real uncertainty becomes "may" or an explicit statement of the uncertainty, never a deleted qualification. A banned neutral word such as "using" that names an instrument becomes "with" or "by" or a restructured clause. A defined technical term on a banned list stays, and you flag the conflict to the user. If no rephrasing preserves the meaning, keep the meaning and say so.
- **Replacing a punctuation mark is a restructuring decision.** When removing a semicolon or em dash, choose the replacement by the relation between the clauses: a full stop for two independent claims, a colon for an explanation or list, a comma or parentheses for an aside, a subordinate clause for a dependency. Check that the replacement does not merge or split claims in a way that changes scope.

## 8. No numerical style goals

Lexical diversity depends on the measure, sample, task, and text length. Predictability depends on the scoring model. Syntax differs across genres and languages. The source reports greater lexical variability in one generation comparison and lower lexical diversity in a revision study, and those concern different operations [19, 21]. A narrow technical topic may properly repeat words and concepts.

Do not target a vocabulary-diversity percentage, a fixed ratio of long to short sentences, or a punctuation frequency. Measurements, if helpful, only locate passages for human review. A long sentence warrants revision when its dependencies are hard to follow, not because it exceeds a count.
