# Target Journal Analysis

## Identify the Journal First

Do not guess. Look for, in order:

1. An explicit statement in project notes, README, cover letter, or correspondence
2. The LaTeX class or Word template (`mdpi.cls`, `IEEEtran.cls`, `elsarticle.cls`, `svjour3.cls`, ACM/ACL styles)
3. The bibliography style and citation format
4. Structural fingerprints: MDPI back-matter sections, IEEE biography blocks, Elsevier highlights, structured abstracts
5. Word and page limits implied by the draft's shape

If it still cannot be determined, state that explicitly, name the two or three journals the manuscript most resembles, and say which evidence points to each. Do not invent a target and then assess fit against it.

## Research the Journal's Current Official Information

From the publisher's own pages, not from memory or third-party summaries:

- Scope and aims
- Accepted article types, and which one this manuscript would be submitted under
- Recent publications and current topic emphasis
- Typical methodological expectations
- Novelty expectations
- Whether conceptual or framework papers are accepted at all
- Whether simulation, analytical, empirical, or experimental work of this type fits
- Submission requirements
- Page or word limits, if applicable
- Required reporting standards or checklists (PRISMA, CONSORT, data-availability, ethics statements)
- Anything that produces a desk rejection

Record the URL and access date for each. Journal policies change; an assessment against a stale scope statement is worse than no assessment.

## Read Recent Comparable Papers

Scope statements describe intent. Published papers describe practice, and the gap between the two is where submissions die.

Select several recent papers from the journal that are genuinely close to this manuscript — same problem class, method family, or evaluation style. For each, note: what kind of contribution earned acceptance, how much validation was expected, what evaluation depth was normal, how novelty was framed, and typical length and structure.

Then state plainly how this manuscript compares to that observed bar.

## Fit Assessment

Score each dimension and justify it against the evidence gathered above.

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| **Scientific / topic fit** | Is the subject matter what this journal publishes? |
| **Methodological fit** | Does the journal publish work using these methods at this rigor? |
| **Novelty fit** | Does the contribution clear this journal's novelty bar? |
| **Evidence / validation fit** | Does the validation match what recent accepted papers provided? |
| **Article-type fit** | Does it map cleanly onto an accepted article type? |
| **Audience fit** | Will this journal's readership find the contribution relevant? |
| **Overall compatibility** | Aggregate, with the binding constraint named |

Name the single binding constraint — the one dimension most likely to cause rejection. That is the actionable output.

## Desk-Rejection Risk Check

Flag each of these explicitly as present, absent, or unknown:

- Out of scope for the journal
- Wrong article type
- Over length limits
- Missing mandatory statements (ethics, data availability, funding, author contributions, conflicts of interest)
- Missing required checklist or reporting standard
- Formatting far from the template
- Incomplete reference list or unresolved citations
- Figures below required resolution or unlicensed for reuse
- Evidence of prior publication or substantial overlap with the authors' own prior work
- Claims of novelty contradicted by literature the journal's own reviewers would know

## Alternative Venues

If overall compatibility is weak, name two or three better-matched journals and say what specifically makes each a better fit — scope, methodological tolerance, novelty bar, or article type. Say what the manuscript would need to change for each. If the current target is appropriate, say so and say why; do not manufacture alternatives.
